# IPv6

:::goal
**Learning goal:** You can explain why IPv6 exists, read IPv6 addresses and write them in short form, tell the most important address types apart, and explain how a device gets an address automatically with IPv6 and finds its neighbours.
:::

In section 4.2.1 you worked out: with 32 bits there are about **4.3 billion** IPv4 addresses — fewer than one per person, even though today everyone has several devices. That's why the free IPv4 addresses have long since been used up. Tricks like NAT (chapter 7) can bridge the gap for a while.

The real solution is **IPv6**. An IPv6 address is **128 bits** long. That gives 2¹²⁸ addresses — a number with 39 digits, about 340 undecillion (3.4 × 10³⁸). Every device on earth can get as many of them as it needs.

Today IPv4 and IPv6 usually run **in parallel**: your phone or your router at home very probably already has both.
