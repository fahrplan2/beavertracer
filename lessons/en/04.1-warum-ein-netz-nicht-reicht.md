# Why one network isn't enough

On the workspace you see two small company networks: **Firma A** (company A) with Anna (`192.168.0.10`) and Ben (`192.168.0.11`), **Firma B** (company B) with Clara (`172.16.0.10`) and David (`172.16.0.11`). The two switches are even connected directly with a cable.

## A ping in your own network

1. Switch to :fa-play: **Run** mode, open the :fa-terminal: **Terminal** on **Anna** and ping Ben:
   ```
   $ ping 192.168.0.11
   ```
2. Switch to :fa-magnifying-glass: **Trace** mode, open a trace for **Anna** and click an `Echo (ping) request`.
3. In the protocol tree, expand the line **Internet Protocol Version 4**. That's the IP part of the packet — the part this chapter is about.

## A ping into the other network

Switch back to :fa-play: **Run** mode and ping Clara from **Anna** (`ping 172.16.0.10`). Then look at Anna's trace once more.

:::quiz mc

## What's behind it

An IP address works similarly to a postal address: one part says which **network** a device is in (like the street), the other part which **device** in that network is meant (like the house number). Anna is in the network `192.168.0…`, Clara in the network `172.16.0…` — two different "streets".

A PC sends packets to devices in its *own* network directly (via ARP and the switch, as you know). For a *foreign* network it needs a device that connects the networks and passes packets on: a **router**. There isn't one here yet — that's why the cable between the switches doesn't help either.

:::tip Key point
The **Internet Protocol** (**IP**) on layer 3 makes sure that packets arrive even between different networks. An **IP address** consists of a **network part** (which network?) and a **host part** (which device?). Devices in the same network reach each other directly — for other networks you need a **router**.
:::
