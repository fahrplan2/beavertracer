# The broadcast storm

:::sim
url=/sims/lesson-3.4.1.btsim
:::

Three switches (A, B, C) are connected in a ring. **Anna** (`192.168.0.10`) is connected to switch A, **Ben** (`192.168.0.11`) to switch B.

## The experiment

1. Switch to :fa-play: **Run** mode, open the :fa-terminal: **Terminal** on **Anna** and ping Ben:
   ```
   $ ping 192.168.0.11
   ```
2. Close Anna's window and watch the workspace for a while: packets keep circling through the ring non-stop.
3. Switch to :fa-magnifying-glass: **Trace** mode and use **+** to open a trace for **Ben**, port `eth0`. At the top right you see how many packets have been recorded so far.
4. Switch back and forth between **Run** and **Trace** a few times and keep an eye on the number.

:::quiz mc
What do you see in Ben's packet list?
- [ ] Anna's pings and Ben's replies, as usual
- [x] The same ARP request "Who has 192.168.0.11?" again and again — and the number of packets keeps growing
- [ ] Nothing at all, the ring blocks everything
:::

:::quiz mc
And what does Anna's terminal report?
- [ ] All pings arrive
- [x] No ping arrives
:::

:::evaluate
Check answers
:::

## What's behind it

Anna's ARP request is a broadcast. Switch A sends it to all ports — so to switch B *and* to switch C. Both pass it on to all their ports again, so also to the other switch and back to A. A passes it on again … The broadcast circles endlessly through the ring, and with every new request more copies are added.

Because the same frames keep arriving at the switches from different directions, their address tables get jumbled up too: one moment Anna is on this port, the next on that one. That's why not even the pings get through any more.

:::tip Key point
If there's a **loop** between switches, broadcasts circle endlessly through the network and multiply — a **broadcast storm**. It brings the network down. Unlike IP packets, Ethernet frames have no "expiry date" that would eventually stop them.
:::
