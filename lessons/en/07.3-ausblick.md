# Outlook: IPv6 and carrier-grade NAT

:::sim
empty
:::

NAT is a brilliant stopgap for the shortage of IPv4 addresses — but a stopgap all the same. There are two developments you should know about.

## IPv6: no more NAT needed

With **IPv6** there are addresses in abundance (chapter 4.6). That's why the provider doesn't give the home router just one address, but a whole **prefix** — often a `/56` or `/48`, i.e. billions of addresses. This is called **prefix delegation** (DHCPv6-PD). From it, the home router hands every device its own **globally unique** address via SLAAC.

NAT is then no longer needed: every device can be addressed directly. That's handy, but it requires a good **firewall** in the router — because the "side effect" of NAT, that nobody can start a connection from outside, disappears. That's what chapter 9 is about.

## Carrier-grade NAT: NAT at the provider

Many providers don't have enough IPv4 addresses themselves. Then not even the home router gets a public address any more, only one from a special range (`100.64.0.0/10`), and the provider does NAT **once more** for many customers together. This is called **carrier-grade NAT** (CGNAT); often that's then the only way for IPv4, while there are real public addresses for IPv6 (*DS-Lite*).

The consequence: a **port forward** in your own router no longer helps with IPv4 — packets from outside already get stuck at the provider. Anyone who wants to run a server at home then needs IPv6 or a different connection.
