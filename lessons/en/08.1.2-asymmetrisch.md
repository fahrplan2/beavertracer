# Asymmetric Encryption

## The idea: an open padlock

Imagine Ben hands out **open padlocks** to everyone who wants to write to him. He keeps the matching **key** to himself.

1. Anna puts her message in a box and snaps one of Ben's padlocks shut.
2. From now on nobody can open the box — not even Anna herself.
3. Only Ben can unlock it again with his key.

Anyone may have the padlock; owning it doesn't help with opening it. That's exactly how **asymmetric encryption** works: every participant has a **key pair**:

| | **Public key** | **Private key** |
|---|---|---|
| corresponds to | the open padlock | the key to the padlock |
| who knows it? | **everyone** — it may be published | **only the owner** — it never leaves their computer |
| what for? | **encrypting** messages **to** the owner | **decrypting** these messages |

The two keys belong together mathematically: what was encrypted with the public key can be decrypted **only** with the matching private key. That solves the key exchange problem: Ben can send his public key around openly — an eavesdropper can't decrypt anything with it.

## Why can't you calculate the private key?

The public key is calculated from the private one. The other way round is practically impossible. With the best-known method, **RSA** (named after Rivest, Shamir and Adleman, 1977), this is based on the fact that **large numbers are very hard to split into their prime factors**: multiplying two huge primes is lightning fast — but recovering the two factors from a product with more than 600 digits is beyond any computer today.

:::note
Besides RSA there are methods based on **elliptic curves** (abbreviated **EC**, e.g. **ECDHE** and **ECDSA**). They rely on a different hard mathematical problem but manage with much shorter keys. The principle — public and private key — is the same. Modern HTTPS connections and BeaverTracer use elliptic curves; you'll see these abbreviations again in the tracer.
:::

## The catch: slow

Asymmetric methods require very elaborate calculations and are about **a thousand times slower** than AES. Encrypting a whole web page or video with them would be far too slow. How to combine both anyway is shown in the section **Hybrid encryption**.

:::tip Key point
With **asymmetric encryption**, everyone has a **key pair**: anyone may know the **public key**; you use it to encrypt messages **to** its owner. Only the owner's **private key** can decrypt them again. Methods like **RSA** rely on large numbers being almost impossible to split into their prime factors. Asymmetric methods solve the key exchange problem but are slow.
:::
