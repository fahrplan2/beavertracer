# Digital Signatures

Encryption gives you **confidentiality**, hashes reveal changes. But how do you prove **who** a message comes from? On paper you sign it. On the net there is the **digital signature**.

## Using the key pair the other way round

When encrypting, the sender uses the recipient's **public** key. With a signature it's the other way round: the sender uses their **own private** key. Since only they own this key, only they can create this signature. Anyone can check it, though — with the sender's **public** key.

**Signing** (Anna signs a contract):

1. Anna calculates the **hash** of the contract.
2. She processes the hash with her **private key**. The result is the **signature**.
3. She sends the contract together with the signature to Ben.

**Verifying** (Ben checks the signature):

1. Ben calculates the hash of the received contract himself.
2. He checks the signature with Anna's **public key**: does it match this hash?
3. If it matches, Ben knows two things:
    - The contract really comes from Anna — only she has the private key (**authenticity**).
    - The contract wasn't changed on the way — otherwise it would have a different hash (**integrity**).

:::note
Why does Anna sign only the hash and not the whole contract? Asymmetric methods are slow. The hash is always short — whether the contract has one page or a thousand.
:::

:::tip Key point
A **digital signature** is created by the sender from the message's **hash** and their **private key**. Anyone can verify it with the sender's **public key**. A valid signature proves **who** the message comes from (**authenticity**) and that it was **not changed** (**integrity**).
:::
