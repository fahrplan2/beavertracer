# Certificates: Whose Key Is This?

With asymmetric encryption and signatures, everything seems solved: your bank's web server sends you its public key, and off you go. But how do you know that this public key **really belongs to the bank**?

An attacker who redirects your connection could simply send you **their own** public key and claim to be the bank. Your browser would then dutifully encrypt for the attacker — who reads everything. Such an attacker in the middle is called a **man-in-the-middle**.

## The certificate: an ID card for keys

In real life you show your **ID card** to prove who you are. People believe the ID because a **trusted authority** issued it — the city administration — and because it's hard to forge.

On the net, a **certificate** does this job. Among other things it contains:

| Field | Meaning | on an ID card |
|---|---|---|
| **Subject** (often `CN=…`) | Who is the certificate for? For web servers: the name, e.g. `CN=www.biberbank.de` | holder's name |
| **Public key** | The holder's key | photo |
| **Issuer** | Who issued the certificate? | issuing authority |
| **Validity** | from … to … | valid until |
| **Signature** of the issuer | Proves that the issuer really issued the certificate like this | seal, hologram |

`CN` stands for *Common Name*.

The authority that issues certificates is called a **Certificate Authority**, **CA** for short. Before it signs a certificate for `www.biberbank.de`, it checks that the applicant really owns this domain.

## Whom does the browser trust?

For every certificate, your browser checks:

1. Is the CA's **signature** genuine? (For that it needs the CA's public key.)
2. Is the certificate still **valid**?
3. Does the **name** in the certificate match the address you entered?

But how does the browser know the CA? Every operating system and browser comes with a **pre-installed** list of about 150 **root CAs**. Their certificates are **self-signed** — they vouch for themselves; you trust them because they ship with the software.

Often a root CA doesn't sign directly but via an **intermediate CA**. This creates a **chain of trust**:

```
Root CA (pre-installed, trusted)
  └─ signs → intermediate CA
                └─ signs → www.biberbank.de
```

The browser follows the chain from bottom to top until it reaches a root CA it trusts. If that fails — or validity or name don't match — it shows a **warning**.

:::tip Key point
A **certificate** confirms that a **public key** belongs to a particular **name**. It is signed by a **Certificate Authority (CA)**. The browser trusts a certificate if it reaches a pre-installed **root CA** through a **chain of trust**, the certificate is **valid** and the **name** matches the address.
:::
